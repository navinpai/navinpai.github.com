AI SRE is sold on MTTR, but nobody shows you the bill. Today we will follow the whole pipeline: deciding whether an alert deserves attention, grouping related alerts, collecting evidence, investigating, and taking action.

My perspective is building systems in this space at StackGen. The numbers in this talk are a transparent worked example, not a customer bill or a measured StackGen benchmark. The question is: which engineering decisions make the bill grow, and which controls reduce spend while preserving useful outcomes?

02 / 19Count failed and abandoned runs in the numerator.

MTTR is useful. It tells us about the time to restore service. It does not describe how many duplicate investigations ran, how much telemetry they scanned, or whether the apparent fix was correct.

Use cost per correctly resolved incident alongside speed. Put all operating spend in the numerator, including failed and abandoned work. Count a resolution only after the outcome is verified. For investigation-only products, use cost per accepted, evidence-backed diagnosis and report the acceptance rate too. Otherwise it is easy to make costs look better by doing less useful work.

For the worked example we will first price model usage. Later we add the human and error-cost dimensions that prevent us from confusing that subtotal with total cost of ownership.

06 / 19*Assumed reduction rates, to be validated against incident recall.

Filtering and grouping solve different problems. First remove repeated notifications and known noise using explicit admission rules. In this example, 60% of 1,000 raw alerts are removed, leaving 400 eligible alerts.

Classification assigns severity, service, and ownership. Grouping then asks which eligible alerts may describe the same incident. Consider checkout errors, payment timeouts, worker retries, and database saturation in the same window. If service topology shows a shared database dependency, they are candidates for one incident group even though their alert text and service names differ.

Investigate that group once with a shared evidence bundle, rather than launch four agents that rediscover the same dependency. Grouping is not merely deduplication, and dependency proximity is not proof of a common cause: preserve each member alert, check timing and evidence, and split the group if the observations diverge. Unknown topology and critical alerts need a safe independent path.

The assumed average is four eligible alerts per group: 400 alerts become 100 investigations. Classification costs 400 × $0.01 = $4/day; grouping decisions cost 400 × $0.04 = $16/day; model usage for investigations costs 100 × $4 = $400/day. Backend and operating charges remain separate. Measure incident recall, false merges, and missed critical incidents.

07 / 19Break-even: avoided investigations must exceed grouping + added error costs.

Four hundred eligible alerts produce 400 × 399 / 2 = 79,800 distinct pairs. Calling a model for each pair at our example $0.04 decision cost would cost $3,192. That is a deliberately naive correlation design, not a claim about how all products work.

Choose candidates with service, environment, time, and dependency information first. Related alerts can span several services: use the shared dependency and time window to propose a group, then investigate its combined evidence. One bounded grouping decision per eligible alert costs 400 × $0.04 = $16. The decision allowance here is 30k input and 2k output on the small model, including candidate descriptions. Indexes, embeddings if used, and runtime have additional costs.

Grouping avoids 300 investigations: 300 × $4 = $1,200 gross, or $1,184 after grouping inference, counting model usage only. Classification is unchanged in both choices. This assumes the same useful incidents survive. Audit false merges and false splits, retain member evidence, and allow a group to split or reopen.

Suppose checkout latency increases. A dependency graph shows payments and its database in the relevant path, and there is a recent payments deployment. That gives us a bounded starting hypothesis and search scope: those services, the incident time range, changes, errors, and traces.

12 / 19Micro-example: 100k input + 10k output · no scans · equal task quality must be tested

Reasoning about conflicting evidence, choosing a tool with valid arguments, classifying an alert, and summarizing a large result are different jobs. Paying for the strongest model and deepest reasoning at every step is a design choice. Model routing is one of the largest available cost levers when simpler tasks dominate volume.

Reasoning may need a stronger model with adaptive effort. Tool selection needs dependable tool-use behavior and schema compliance; the runtime executes the tool and enforces permissions. Classification often works with a smaller model plus an explicit unknown path. Summarization needs faithfulness and preserved anomalies, not merely short output. These are capability profiles, not assertions that any model tier automatically meets a task's quality bar.

For a separate micro-example, 100k input and 10k output at $5/$25 costs $0.75. Move 80% of both token types to a $1/$5 model: $0.12 small-model work plus $0.15 premium work. Add a $0.01 small-model routing decision: $0.28 total, 62.7% lower. It assumes unchanged token workload and successful routing. More retries, reasoning, or escalations can erase the saving. Compare cost per accepted result and per correctly resolved incident on end-to-end evaluations.

14 / 19Hypothetical risk, not measured accuracy · 5 min of review at $120/hour = $10

The action planning and verification tokens might cost $1.50. If, hypothetically, a harmful action has a 1% chance of causing a $10,000 loss, its expected loss is $100 per attempt. The probability and impact are scenario assumptions, not observed AI or StackGen failure rates. Real incidents have a distribution of impacts, including tail risks.

A human review also has a cost: five minutes at an assumed loaded rate of $120 per hour is $10. That does not prove review is always worth it; compare how much it actually reduces error and whether its delay increases incident impact. The same expected-loss logic applies to a missed alert or a false grouping decision.

Check preconditions against live state. Require appropriate approval for high-impact changes. Make execution idempotent, verify recovery independently, and retain rollback options. A model saying it fixed the issue is not an outcome metric. Savings must be evaluated together with missed incidents, diagnosis accuracy, harmful actions, and time to verified recovery.

15 / 19Conceptual curves, not measured savings · stable workload + validated reuse · drift can raise costs

The graph is a design expectation, not production data or a fitted forecast. With a stable workload, validated reusable lessons should reduce cost per investigation as the system becomes familiar with services, dependencies, query scopes, and prior failures. Repeat incidents benefit most: the system can retrieve a tested investigation path rather than discover it again. The overall average falls more slowly because some incidents are new.

The horizontal axis is accumulated verified investigations retained as useful lessons, not simply elapsed time. The vertical axis is a normalized relative cost index. Both curves settle above zero. Live-state checks, current evidence retrieval, recovery verification, and allocated memory maintenance still cost money; novel incidents keep the overall floor higher. Workload drift, stale topology, or a bad memory policy can raise costs again. No numeric saving or convergence time is promised.

Build the loop explicitly: link the diagnosis to a verified outcome and corrections; extract scoped lessons with source evidence; turn them into regression checks; version, index, and expire them; retrieve relevant lessons before planning the next investigation; verify they apply to current state. Retain failed hypotheses as negative evidence too. This can be retrieval and workflow memory, without changing model weights.

Learning itself costs tokens, indexing, review, storage, and invalidation. Charge those costs to the pipeline and amortize them across demonstrated reuse. Track repeated-incident cohorts and stable severity mixes, and compare cost at fixed recall and recovery quality. A shorter answer is not proof that the system learned. The feedback infrastructure is a continuing part of running an AI SRE system.

Start by eliminating duplicate work, then reduce the evidence each run has to buy and read, then route models by task and enforce severity budgets. Profiling may change the order. Budgets and recovery verification exist from the start; the ranking is an optimization sequence.

Make the feedback loop part of every control. Store verified fixes, failed hypotheses, useful query scopes, and regression cases. Evaluate reuse and expire stale lessons. A system that investigates the same failure from scratch forever never captures that operating leverage.

For each change, measure incident recall, evidence-backed diagnosis, verified recovery, harmful actions, and MTTR by severity. Lower spend that quietly loses serious incidents is not an improvement.
